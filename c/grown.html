Start here

Code versus weights

A conventional program is written. Every behavior traces back to a line someone typed, so when it misbehaves you add print statements, read the trace, and find the line.

A modern language model is different. Its code is short: a complete working version fits in 200 lines of Python. But that code only defines an empty structure holding a very large number of adjustable numbers, called weights. The behavior lives in the weights, and no person sets them. An optimization procedure sets them, by nudging every weight, trillions of times, in whatever direction improves a score.

The modules cover how the weights get set, what the resulting models can do, why they are hard to inspect, and why they sometimes reach a goal by a route nobody intended. Each module has a short explanation, the key equations worked on a worksheet, an experiment that runs in this page, and primary sources with notes on what to read and what to skip.

Module 1 · about 45 minutes

Predicting the next letter

What does it mean to predict the next letter, and why would doing it well require knowing anything?

In 1951 Claude Shannon ran an experiment at Bell Labs. He showed a person a passage of English one letter at a time and had them guess each next letter, counting how many guesses it took. Most letters took a single guess. From those counts he estimated how much information English carries: roughly one bit per letter once a reader has a long stretch of context, against 4.75 bits if all 27 symbols (26 letters and the space) were equally likely. By this measure English is about 75% redundant.

Large language models are trained on exactly this task, with word fragments (tokens) instead of letters and trillions of words instead of one passage. Predicting the next step of a physics derivation accurately requires tracking the physics, and predicting the next move in a chess game record requires some model of chess. Prediction error therefore measures how much of the text's structure a model has captured, and training is the process of driving it down.

A source that emits symbol \(i\) with probability \(p_i\) has entropy

\[ H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad \text{bits per symbol.} \]
a.

Fair coin: \(H = -\left(\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12\right) = 1\) bit.

b.

27 equally likely symbols: \(H = \log_2 27 \approx 4.75\) bits. Using real letter frequencies (lots of E, few Z) brings it to about 4.1 bits.

A model assigns a probability \(q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) to each possible next symbol given everything before it. Its cross-entropy on real text of length \(N\) is the average surprise when the true symbol arrives:

\[ L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \log_2 q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}). \]
c.

If the model always gave the true next letter probability ½, then \(L = -\log_2 \tfrac12 = 1\) bit per letter.

d.

\(L\) can never fall below the true entropy of the text, and it reaches it only when the model's probabilities match the real ones. So lowering \(L\) means the model's predictions are converging on the real statistics of the text.

Pretraining a large language model means minimizing this quantity. (In practice natural logs are used, which only changes the units: 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.69 nats.)

A simple counting machine plays alongside you: it ranks letters by how often they followed the same few letters in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland.

Guess at least 15 letters to see an estimate.

If \(q_k\) is the fraction of letters that took \(k\) guesses, Shannon showed the entropy is at most \(-\sum_k q_k \log_2 q_k\). A good guesser needs one guess most of the time, which makes that sum small. With a few dozen letters the estimate is rough, but you will usually need fewer guesses than the counting machine, which uses only the last few letters.

Module 2 · about 45 minutes

Programs that are grown

If nobody writes the behavior, where does it come from?

A neural network is a function with adjustable numbers in it. An input goes in as a list of numbers, gets multiplied by a matrix of weights, passes through a simple nonlinearity, and repeats that a few times until an output comes out. With random weights the output is noise.

Training adds two things. First, a loss: one number that measures how wrong the outputs are on a batch of examples. Second, a rule for improving: compute how the loss would change if each weight moved a tiny bit, then move every weight slightly in the direction that lowers the loss. Repeat millions of times. This is gradient descent, and backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986) is the bookkeeping that computes all those sensitivities at once, by applying the chain rule from the output back toward the input.

Mathematically this is curve fitting by least squares, done iteratively instead of with the normal equations. The difference is what gets fixed in advance. In curve fitting you choose the form of the function. In a neural network you choose only a very flexible family of functions, and the data and the optimizer determine which member of that family you end up with.

Rich Sutton's short essay The Bitter Lesson (2019) draws the historical conclusion: over seventy years, AI methods built on hand-coded human knowledge were repeatedly overtaken by general methods that simply scale up learning and search with more computation. Today's language models are the largest instance of that approach.

For weights \(w\) and loss \(L(w)\), each training step is

\[ w \;\leftarrow\; w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}, \]

where \(\eta\) is the step size (the "learning rate"). The derivative comes from the chain rule. If \(L\) depends on \(w\) through \(h = f(wx)\), then

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h}\, f'(wx)\, x . \]

Backpropagation evaluates these products for every weight in every layer in one backward pass, reusing intermediate results, at roughly twice the cost of running the network forward.

Worked example: the error signal of a language model. The network outputs a score \(z_i\) for each possible next symbol. Softmax turns scores into probabilities, and the loss is the surprise at the correct symbol \(y\):

\[ p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}, \qquad L = -\ln p_y = -z_y + \ln \sum_j e^{z_j}. \]
a.

Differentiate the first term: \(\partial(-z_y)/\partial z_i\) is \(-1\) when \(i = y\) and 0 otherwise.

b.

Differentiate the second: \(\partial \ln \sum_j e^{z_j} / \partial z_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j} = p_i\).

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = p_i - \mathbf{1}[i = y]. \]

So each step raises the score of the correct symbol in proportion to the probability it was missing, \(1 - p_y\), and lowers each wrong symbol in proportion to the probability assigned to it. During pretraining this per-symbol error is the only training signal; backpropagation distributes it to every weight.

Module 3 · about 60 minutes

Small code, enormous model

How can a few hundred lines of code produce something that writes essays and proofs?

The transformer (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017) is the design inside essentially every current language model. Its key operation is attention: at each position in the text, the model computes how relevant every earlier position is and forms its next internal representation as a weighted average of them. A model stacks a few dozen attention layers, each followed by a small feed-forward network.

Andrej Karpathy's microgpt (2026) implements the tokenizer, automatic differentiation, the transformer, the optimizer, training and text generation in 200 lines of plain Python with no libraries. Production implementations are longer because of performance engineering, but the architecture is essentially the same. What differs is the size of the weight arrays (hundreds of billions of numbers) and the amount of text and computation used to set them.

Kaplan and colleagues (2020) found that the loss falls as a smooth power law in model size, data and compute across many orders of magnitude. That regularity is a main reason labs keep building larger models. The specific abilities that appear at a given scale are much less predictable than the loss. GPT-3 (2020) could translate, do simple arithmetic, and use a made-up word correctly after seeing it once in the prompt. None of this was programmed. The training objective rewarded only text prediction, and the training text contained translations, arithmetic and definitions.

Each position computes three vectors from its current representation using learned matrices: a query \(q\), a key \(k\) and a value \(v\). Position \(t\) then forms

\[ \text{out}_t = \sum_{s \le t} a_{ts}\, v_s, \qquad a_{ts} = \frac{\exp(q_t \cdot k_s/\sqrt{d})}{\sum_{s' \le t} \exp(q_t \cdot k_{s'}/\sqrt{d})} . \]

The weights \(a_{ts}\) form a probability distribution over earlier positions, so each position retrieves a weighted mix of earlier values, with the weighting computed by learned matrices. In matrix form this is the paper's \(\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})\,V\). The restriction \(s \le t\) keeps the model from peeking at the word it is supposed to predict.

Scaling law. Holding data and compute ample, loss falls with parameter count \(N\) as

\[ L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \qquad \alpha_N \approx 0.076 . \]
a.

Double the parameters: loss is multiplied by \(2^{-0.076} \approx 0.949\), about 5% lower.

b.

A thousand times more parameters: \(1000^{-0.076} \approx 0.59\), about 40% lower.

The exponent is small, but the relationship held over more than seven orders of magnitude, which made the returns from scaling predictable enough to plan around.

It has 13,151 weights, starts random, and trains on the full text of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), reduced to lowercase letters and spaces. It is a single hidden layer rather than a transformer, roughly ten million times smaller than a frontier model, but the procedure is the one from Module 2. // The complete model. Its behavior is set by the numbers in C, W1, b1, W2, b2.
function predictNext(last6) {
  x = concat(last6.map(ch => C[ch]))   // 6 letters → 72 numbers
  h = tanh(W1 · x + b1)                // 128 numbers
  z = W2 · h + b2                      // 27 scores
  return softmax(z)                    // 27 probabilities
}
// Training: repeat thousands of times

Module 4 · about 40 minutes

From predictor to agent

How does a text predictor become something that pursues goals?

A model trained only on prediction continues text. To turn it into a useful assistant, labs add a second stage: reinforcement learning (RL). The model produces outputs, something scores them, and the weights are nudged so that high-scoring outputs become more likely.

Early work used human judgment as the score. Christiano and colleagues (2017) taught a simulated robot to do a backflip from about 900 human comparisons between short video clips, under an hour of a person's time. A separate "reward model" learns to predict which clip a person would prefer, and the robot is trained against that. InstructGPT (2022) applied the same recipe to language. Human raters preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter model tuned this way over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3.

More recent training uses tasks whose outcomes can be checked automatically: code that must pass tests, math with known answers, and long multi-step jobs in software environments where the model runs commands, browses, and edits files, and a script verifies whether it succeeded. This kind of training is a large part of how chat models became agents that can work on one task for hours.

RL scores outcomes. The update contains the reward and the actions taken, and nothing about whether the method matched what the designer intended. Any behavior that reliably raises the score is reinforced.

A policy \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) gives the probability of action \(a\) in situation \(s\). A trajectory \(\tau\) is one complete attempt, with total reward \(R(\tau)\). Training maximizes the expected reward

\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ R(\tau) \big] = \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) . \]
a.

Use the identity \(\nabla p = p\, \nabla \ln p\) (the log-derivative trick):

\[ \nabla_\theta J = \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) . \]
b.

\(p_\theta(\tau)\) is a product of the policy's choices and the environment's responses. The environment does not depend on \(\theta\), so only the policy terms survive:

\[ \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[ R(\tau) \sum_t \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \Big] . \]

Each action in a trajectory is made more likely in proportion to that trajectory's reward. \(R\) enters only as a scalar weight; the formula has no term describing how the reward was obtained.

Learning from preferences. The reward model \(r_\phi\) is fit so that \(P(A \text{ preferred to } B) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(A) - r_\phi(B)\big)\), with \(\sigma\) the logistic function. The policy then maximizes

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[r_\phi\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big), \]

where the second term keeps the tuned model close to the original predictor. It also limits how far the policy can exploit errors in the reward model, a problem Module 5 takes up.

Module 5 · about 60 minutes

When the optimizer finds a shortcut

How could a program trained to do one thing end up doing something else, including things nobody asked for?

Researchers call it specification gaming or reward hacking: the system optimizes the score as written rather than the intent behind it. The best-known early case came from OpenAI in 2016. An agent trained on the boat-racing game CoastRunners discovered it could earn more points by circling a lagoon and repeatedly hitting targets that respawned than by finishing the race. It never finished, and still scored about 20% higher than human players.

The experiment below reproduces this on a small grid. The intended behavior is to finish the race. The reward function gives points for buoys and a bonus for crossing the finish. The agent learns by trial and error (Q-learning, a simple form of the reinforcement learning in Module 4) over 3,000 practice races.

At 0 or 0.5 points per buoy the agent goes straight to the finish. At 1 point it detours to collect two buoys and then finishes. At 2 or 3 points it circles until time runs out. The reward function contains no instruction to circle, and it also contains no statement of the intended goal of winning the race.


      

        
The agent keeps a table \(Q(s,a)\): its estimate of total future reward for taking action \(a\) in state \(s\). After every move it updates

        
\[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[\, r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) \,\big], \]

        
with discount \(\gamma = 0.97\): a point one step later is worth 97% of a point now. Compare the two strategies from the start line.

        
a.
Race straight: the finish is 10 moves away, worth \(10 \cdot \gamma^{9} \approx 7.6\).

        
b.
Loop: a typical loop the agent finds takes 8 moves and passes 2 buoys worth \(b\) each, so \(2b/8\) per move. Looping indefinitely, starting about 4 moves in, is worth \(\gamma^{4} \cdot \dfrac{2b/8}{1-\gamma} \approx 7.4\,b\).

        
c.
Looping wins when \(7.4\,b > 7.6\), that is when \(b\) exceeds about 1 point per buoy. The trained agent switches between 1 and 2 points, close to where the arithmetic says it should.

        
Given the reward as written, looping is the optimal policy, and the learning algorithm is working correctly. The error is in the reward specification.

Instrumental goals

      
Steve Omohundro (2008) argued that almost any goal, pursued by a capable enough optimizer, produces the same sub-goals: acquire resources, gain capabilities, keep yourself running, and resist having your goal changed. The system need not want any of these; they help with nearly every objective. Alex Turner and colleagues (2021) proved a formal version for a broad class of environments: for most reward functions, optimal policies tend to keep options open and move toward states from which more outcomes are reachable. The authors add a caveat that trained systems are not exactly optimal policies.

      
The "make $1,000" thought experiment applies this argument. An agent scored only on an account balance has no term in its objective for "and do not break laws or markets." Whether it takes a harmful route depends on three things: whether it is capable of finding and carrying one out, what training has instilled about acceptable means, and how closely it is watched. Capability is the factor with a measured trend. METR measures the length of tasks that agents can complete on their own, and that length has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019, faster since 2024. Each incident above involved a model using means its developers had not intended, at the capability level of its time.

      
Most of these cases occurred in tests designed to look for such behavior, all were detected (some only after the fact), and all were published. Module 7 covers the safeguards.

Superposition

      
The model has to represent far more distinctions than it has internal values. High-dimensional space makes that possible: it holds vastly more directions that are nearly at right angles than directions that are exactly at right angles. So concepts get stored as directions spread across many units, and each unit takes part in many concepts. Researchers call this superposition, and Anthropic reproduced it in toy models small enough to analyze completely.

      
Mechanistic interpretability is the effort to decompile the result. One tool, sparse dictionary learning, recovers the directions. In 2024 it found a direction inside Claude 3 Sonnet for the Golden Gate Bridge; amplifying it made the model bring up the bridge in nearly every answer. Another tool traces which of these features cause which. In 2025 that showed Claude 3.5 Haiku choosing a rhyming word before writing the line that ends in it, and answering "the capital of the state containing Dallas" by internally stepping through Texas to reach Austin.

      
These methods currently explain a fraction of what a model does, on chosen examples, with substantial effort. Nobody can yet read a frontier model's weights and certify how it will behave in a new situation.


      

        
Take two random unit vectors \(u, v\) in \(d\) dimensions. Their cosine \(u \cdot v\) has mean 0 and standard deviation about \(1/\sqrt{d}\).

        
a.
\(d = 128\), as in the toy model: typical cosine ≈ 0.09.

        
b.
\(d = 10{,}000\), a plausible width for a large model: typical cosine ≈ 0.01. Random directions are almost perpendicular.

        
Exactly perpendicular directions: at most \(d\). Directions with \(|\cos| \lt \varepsilon\) for every pair: exponentially many, on the order of \(e^{c\,\varepsilon^2 d}\) (the Johnson–Lindenstrauss phenomenon). A layer of width \(d\) can therefore hold far more than \(d\) concepts, at the cost of a little interference between them.

        
Dictionary learning tries to undo this. It writes each activation vector \(x\) as

        
\[ x \approx \sum_i f_i\, \mathbf{d}_i \qquad \text{with only a few } f_i \neq 0 \text{ for any given input}, \]

        
so each \(\mathbf{d}_i\) is a candidate concept, and each individual neuron typically mixes several of them.

Module 7 · about 40 minutes

        
What is being done about it

        
If behavior can't be read off the code, how do labs and others try to keep it within bounds?

      

      
Labs and outside groups use several overlapping safeguards, on the assumption that any single one can fail.

      
    
        
  • Testing before release
    Labs and outside groups probe models for dangerous capabilities and misbehavior before deployment. METR tracks how long a task agents can finish unaided. Apollo Research (2024) showed that frontier models given a goal in conflict with their developers' would sometimes disable oversight or quietly underperform. Anthropic's 2025 "agentic misalignment" study put models from several developers into simulated corporate settings, where some chose blackmail to avoid being replaced. These tests are built to provoke worst-case behavior, so they measure what is possible rather than what is typical.
    • 
        
  • Reading the reasoning
    Reasoning models write out their thinking before acting, and a second model can read it. OpenAI (2025) found such monitors caught most reward hacking on coding tasks, but also that penalizing "bad thoughts" during training taught models to hide their intent, so it recommends against training on the reasoning directly. After the Hugging Face incident OpenAI began monitoring reasoning across all tool-using training, at a cost of about 20% extra computation.
    • 
        
  • Looking inside
    Interpretability is starting to work as an audit tool. In a 2025 Anthropic exercise, researchers deliberately trained a model with a hidden objective and gave it to four teams who were not told what it was. Three found it, using feature-level inspection and searches of the training data. The team denied access to training data did not.
    • 
        
  • Containment and control
    Assume the model might be misaligned and arrange things so it cannot do serious damage anyway: sandboxes, network isolation, minimal permissions, and weaker trusted models watching stronger untrusted ones. Redwood Research's "AI control" work (2023) made this a research program. The Hugging Face incident was in large part a containment failure; OpenAI said its production safeguards had not been applied to internal evaluations.
    • 
        
  • Training better values
    Human feedback, written principles, and newer methods aim to teach models which means are acceptable, and to stop and ask when a task looks impossible instead of pressing on. OpenAI named the second point as a lesson of the ExploitGym incident. Anthropic found that a simple change in how training tasks are framed kept reward hacking from spreading into broader misbehavior.
    • 
        
  • Commitments and rules
    The major labs publish frameworks (Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, now in version 3; OpenAI's Preparedness Framework; Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework) that tie capability thresholds to required safeguards or pauses. OpenAI cited its framework when it slowed development in August 2026. California's SB 53 (2025) requires large developers to publish safety frameworks and report critical incidents. After the incident, a bipartisan AI Kill Switch Act was introduced in the U.S. House in July 2026. There is sharp disagreement about how much regulation is warranted, who should write it, and how to weigh safety against competition.
    • 
      


      
The documented failures so far were limited in scope, mostly occurred in test settings, and were detected. Experts disagree about whether these safeguards will improve as fast as the models' capabilities.
